                                              Abstract
        A configuration of a cellular automaton on the d-dimensional torus Zdn is said to be shift-
    symmetric if it is fixed by some nonzero translation. For the two-dimensional case, Banda,
    Caughman, Cenek, and Teuscher (Chaos 29, 063120, 2019) enumerated these configurations,
    bounded their number, and offered several dynamical and algorithmic consequences, leaving
    the d-dimensional case open. In this article, we generalize the results to arbitrary dimension.
    Specifically, we obtain an explicit closed-form enumeration of shift-symmetric configurations
    on Zdn for arbitrary side length n, dimension d, and alphabet Σ of size σ. In comparison
    with the two-dimensional case, several key coefficients, namely 1, p+1, and p, are replaced
                      v  
    by rd (p, v) = p(2) vd p , which are the Möbius numbers of the subspace lattice of Fdp weighted
    by Gaussian binomials. We also extend the enumeration to configurations with a prescribed
    number of active cells. The case where n is prime recovers, in complement form, a result of
    Castillo-Ramirez and Gadouleau on aperiodic configurations; the multi-prime closed form,
    and all dynamical consequences, are new. We further prove a lower bound that is attained
    exactly when n is prime, extending the two-dimensional tightness characterization of Banda
    et al. to arbitrary dimension, and upper bounds whose union form sharpens, on tori with
    side n ≥ 7 and at matching exponent, the prefactor of the known general-group bounds, with

    closed form (2d − 1 + 2 log2 n) σ n /2 . Two crossover laws follow: the symmetry probability
                     d
    decays as σ −c n with rate c → 1−1/p1 (n) governed solely by the smallest prime factor of n,
    and the composite-versus-prime symmetry advantage familiar from two dimensions inverts
    within an explicit window around a pair-dependent dimension dc (p) = ln(2 − 2/p)/ ln(1 +
    1/p) ≈ p ln 2. Under the necessary hypothesis of translation-equivariant neighborhoods (a
    counterexample shows uniformity of the local rule alone does not suffice), shift-symmetry is
    irreversible, the per-prime symmetry ranks are non-decreasing along every trajectory, and
    non-symmetric tasks are unsolvable from symmetric inputs. Finally we give a detection
    algorithm for shift-symmetry with worst-case cost O(sopf d (n) · nd ) cell operations and an
    exact average-case analysis. All enumerative claims have been verified against definition-level
    exhaustive computation on small lattices and against independent subgroup-level evaluators
    throughout. The mathematical content of every numbered statement is, moreover, formally
    verified in the Lean 4 proof assistant, and the correspondence between each theorem, lemma,
    proposition, and corollary and its formal statement was audited by hand with a purpose-built
    paper–Lean viewer.

1       Introduction
A configuration of a cellular automaton on the d-dimensional torus Zdn is said to be shift-
symmetric if it is fixed by some nonzero translation (Figure 1). For the two-dimensional case,
Banda, Caughman, Cenek, and Teuscher (Chaos 29, 063120, 2019) studied shift-symmetry for
configurations of two-dimensional cellular automata (CA) on toroidal lattices Zn × Zn . Such
symmetry is noteworthy since, once a configuration is fixed by a nonzero translation, no uniform
synchronous update can ever cause the symmetry to be lost. As a result, symmetric configura-
tions effectively function as irreversible traps that can make non-symmetric tasks (leader elec-
tion, pattern recognition) unsolvable in the worst case. Quantifying this obstruction requires
counting the shift-symmetric configurations, which Banda et al. [1] accomplished for d = 2,
leaving higher-dimensional extensions for future consideration. In this paper, we provide the
d-dimensional extension and, in the process, sharpen and correct several statements whose naive
generalizations to higher dimensions fail.

Contributions. The primary contributions of this paper are the following:

    (i) An explicit closed-form enumeration of the set Snd of shift-symmetric configurations on Zdn
        for arbitrary n, d, and alphabet size σ (Theorem 4.4), with two independent derivations,
        and a refinement to configurations with exactly k active cells (Theorem 5.1).

 (ii) A lower bound for |Snd | attained exactly when n is prime (Theorem 6.1). We show that a
      naive generalization of the two-dimensional bound of [1] is false for d ≥ 3 (Remark 6.2).
      We also give an upper bound with an explicit prefactor 2d − 1 + 2 log2 n (Theorem 6.3).

 (iii) Proofs of irreversibility, insolvability, and per-prime symmetry-rank monotonicity in d
       dimensions (Section 8), under a translation-equivariance hypothesis that we show is neces-
       sary: a two-cell counterexample (Remark 2.5) demonstrates that a shared local rule with
       cell-dependent neighborhoods does not preserve symmetry.

 (iv) A detection algorithm for shift-symmetry with worst-case bound O(sopf d (n) · nd ) and an
      exact average-case expectation analysis (Section 9), with the average-case “polylogarith-
      mic” claims of the two-dimensional literature restated under appropriate hypotheses.

 (v) A number of computational observations regarding higher dimensions (Section 10): the
     prime/composite extremal pattern of d = 2 breaks for d ≥ 3, and the multi-term correction
     regime collapses rapidly with growing p and d.

Related work. For the two-dimensional torus, Banda et al. [1] enumerated shift-symmetric
configurations, bounded their number, and analyzed detection; to our knowledge no extension
has appeared since. From the group-theoretic side, Castillo-Ramirez and Gadouleau [2] counted
configurations with a given stabilizer class over an arbitrary finite group via the Möbius func-
tion of the subgroup lattice. When combined with Hall’s evaluation of the Möbius function
of a p-group ([6]; see Hawkes, Isaacs, and Özaydin [5, Cor. 3.5]), their Lemma 7 yields, in
complement form, the prime-lattice case of our enumeration theorem, and Section 4.3 shows
how the general case can alternatively be derived from their framework. What appears neither
there nor in the least-period counts of Castillo-Ramirez and Sánchez-Álvarez [3], whose worked
examples stop at rank two, is the explicit multi-prime closed form for Zdn , the prime-tightness
characterization, or any cellular-automata consequences. On the combinatorial side, Ethier [8]
and Ethier and Lee [9] count two-dimensional toroidal arrays up to symmetry (orbits, not sym-
metric configurations), and Adamson, Deligkas, Gusev, and Potapov [7] study multidimensional
necklaces algorithmically, counting the “atranslational” ones — the orbit-level complement of
our shift-symmetric configurations — by a recursion across dimensions; no closed form, prime
structure, or dynamical consequence is derived there. In symbolic dynamics, points fixed by
a given finite-index subgroup of Zd are classical and are packaged by Lind’s zeta function for
Zd -actions [10]; our object is, instead, the union over all nontrivial translations on a fixed finite
torus. Lower bounds on the number of aperiodic configurations of a general finite group were
given by Gao, Jackson, and Seward [4, Cor. 1.7.2] and sharpened in [2, Thm. 5]; specialized
to Zdn , the former is exponentially weaker in the exponent and the latter polynomially weaker
in the prefactor (for n ≥ 7) than the bounds proved here. For d = 1 our formulas reduce
to classical aperiodic-word counts going back to Moreau and Witt [17, 18]; see also [16]. The
prime-lattice, binary, two-dimensional case of our count also appears, without proof, as a for-
mula line in OEIS entry A323863 [11] (orbit-level companion entries: A323872, A184271). On
the dynamical side, Kornyak [12] observed that deterministic discrete dynamics cannot decrease
symmetry; Theorem 8.1 makes the translation-stabilizer version of this precise for equivariant
CA on Zdn .

2    Model
In this section we introduce some of the basic definitions and notation we will use in our study
of cellular automata and their configurations.
Definition 2.1 (Lattice and configurations). Fix integers n ≥ 2, d ≥ 1 and a finite alphabet Σ
with σ = |Σ| ≥ 2 (for counting statements, σ = 1 is also allowed). We define the lattice A to
be the finite abelian group A = (Z/nZ)d , and we refer to the N = nd elements of A as the cells
of A. A configuration is any function s : A → Σ.
Definition 2.2 (Translations acting on configurations). For v ∈ A, the translation τv acts on
the set of configurations by (τv s)(u) = s(u + v), for any u, v ∈ A. For any configuration s, the
corresponding stabilizer, denoted Stab(s) = {v ∈ A : τv s = s}, is a subgroup of A. For any
subgroup H ≤ A, its fixed set is given by Fix(H) = {s : τh s = s for all h ∈ H}.
Definition 2.3 (Shift-symmetry). For 0 ̸= v ∈ A, we define

                                 Snd (v) = {s : τv s = s} = Fix(⟨v⟩).

Definition 2.4 (Equivariant cellular automaton). A cellular automaton on A over Σ is
given by a finite list of offsets o1 , . . . , ob ∈ A and a local rule ϕ : Σb → Σ; the global map
Φ : ΣA → ΣA updates all cells synchronously:
                                                                        
                              Φ(s)(u) = ϕ s(u + o1 ), . . . , s(u + ob ) .

Equivalently, Φ is translation-equivariant: Φ(τv s) = τv Φ(s) for all v ∈ A.
Remark 2.5 (Equivariance is necessary, not cosmetic). We note that the property of translation-
equivariance is used extensively in our discussion of dynamical results. Indeed, definitions in
the style of “a neighborhood function η : A × ΣA → Σb together with a shared local rule ϕ” do
not imply equivariance and do not support the irreversibility theorem, because η may depend
on the cell arbitrarily.
     Here is a counterexample for state-valued η (d = 1, n = 2, Σ = {0, 1}, b = 1): let η0 (s) = (s0 ),
η1 (s) = (0), and ϕ = id. All cells share ϕ, yet the shift-symmetric configuration (1, 1) maps to
(1, 0), which is not shift-symmetric.
     If one insists that η only select locations (each cell reads actual cells, just not translation-
uniform ones), equivariance can still fail on every A, and some location-selecting map breaks
irreversibility exactly when |A| = nd is not prime (on Zp , p prime, every shift-symmetric config-
uration is constant and is mapped to a constant one). In d = 1 the smallest case is n = 4: with
ϕ = id, let every cell read itself except that cell 2 reads cell 1; then (0, 1, 0, 1), fixed by the shift
2, maps to (0, 1, 1, 1), which is not.
     The original paper by Banda et al. [1] considers the two-dimensional case and avoids this
pitfall only inside its proofs, where the neighborhood is explicitly assumed to be given by shared
relative offsets [1, Lemma A.5]. Our Definition 2.4 in this paper makes the hypothesis explicit
from the start.

8    Dynamics: irreversibility, insolvability, rank monotonicity
In this section we establish the dynamical consequences of shift-symmetry in configurations.

Theorem 8.1 (Irreversibility). Let Φ be translation-equivariant (Definition 2.4). Then, for
every configuration s, Stab(Φ(s)) ⊇ Stab(s); in particular, s ∈ Snd (v) implies Φ(s) ∈ Snd (v),
and Φ(Snd ) ⊆ Snd .

Proof. If τv s = s, then τv Φ(s) = Φ(τv s) = Φ(s).

   By Remark 2.5, the equivariance hypothesis cannot be weakened to the assumption “all cells
share the local rule.”

Corollary 8.2 (Insolvability). Let σ ≥ 2 and N > 1. Any task requiring, on some input, an
output configuration outside Snd reachable from an input inside Snd is unsolvable by translation-
equivariant CA on Zdn . Indeed, symmetric inputs exist (all constants lie in Snd ), and by The-
orem 8.1 no trajectory leaves Snd . This covers leader election (the target configuration with a
unique distinguished cell is asymmetric for N > 1); d-dimensional leader-election algorithms as
in [13, 14] therefore necessarily break one of the hypotheses (uniformity, determinism, synchrony)
or restrict the admissible inputs, e.g. to asymmetric configurations. More generally, since Stab
can only grow along a trajectory (Theorem 8.1), every pattern-recognition or image-processing
task whose required output breaks a symmetry of some admissible input is unsolvable, as in the
two-dimensional discussion of [1].

Proposition 8.3 (Per-primerank monotonicity). For a prime p | n define the p-rank of s as
rkp (s) = dimFp Stab(s)∩A[p] ∈ {0, . . . , d} (via Lemma 3.3). Under any translation-equivariant
Φ, every p-rank is non-decreasing along every trajectory: rkp (Φ(s)) ≥ rkp (s) for all p | n simul-
taneously.

Proof. By Theorem 8.1, Stab(Φ(s)) ∩ A[p] ⊇ Stab(s) ∩ A[p], and both are Fp -subspaces.

    This replaces the informal “symmetry rank (for the dominant prime)” of earlier drafts: for

Applications. The counts use only a finite alphabet and a torus, and the dynamical results
use only translation equivariance (their proofs do not need a finite alphabet), so they apply
wherever these ingredients occur.

• Distributed computing. A CA on Zdn is a synchronous anonymous network (a torus), a special
  case of the networks for which Angluin proved the classical impossibility of symmetry breaking
  [21]; Yamashita and Kameda characterized when leader election and related problems are
  solvable on anonymous networks [22]. Corollary 8.2 is the corresponding statement for CA,
  and the counts make it quantitative: for N > 1, every translation-equivariant CA fails at
  leader election on all inputs in Snd , a fraction Pnunif
                                                       d   of all inputs, bounded in Corollary 7.1.

• Machine learning. A stride-one convolutional layer with circular padding, shared weights, and
  pointwise nonlinearities is a translation-equivariant map of feature maps on Zdn [23]. Since the
  proof of Theorem 8.1 uses equivariance alone, no stack of such layers can remove a translation
  symmetry of its input, an instance of the limitation of equivariant networks discussed by
  Kaba and Ravanbakhsh [24]. Neural cellular automata [25] grow patterns from a single seed
  cell, which is an asymmetric configuration, and update their cells at random; in the terms of
  Corollary 8.2, they restrict the inputs and drop determinism and synchrony.

• Materials science. Enumerations of the derivative superstructures of an alloy discard the
  “super-periodic” labelings, whose period is shorter than the supercell [26]. For a Bravais parent
  lattice, σ atomic species, and the supercell spanned by n times the parent basis vectors, these
  are exactly the configurations of Snd (d = 3 for bulk crystals), so Theorem 4.4 counts them,
  and for binary alloys Theorem 5.1 counts them at fixed composition. A general supercell has
  a quotient of the form Za × Zb × Zc (Smith normal form), a rectangular torus as in open
  problem (2) below.

• Physics. In exact diagonalization of quantum lattice models with translation symmetry, basis
  states whose period is shorter than the lattice need a modified normalization and are compat-
  ible with only some momenta [27, §§4.1.3, 4.4.1]; on Zdn with σ local states, these are exactly
  the configurations of Snd . More generally, by the argument of Theorem 8.1, any determinis-
  tic update map on a periodic grid that commutes with the grid translations (for example, a
  synchronous update applying the same rule at every site, as in a lattice gas or an explicit dis-
  cretization of a reaction–diffusion equation) preserves, in exact arithmetic, every translation
  symmetry of its initial data; symmetric initial data must therefore be perturbed (round-off
  can act as such a perturbation) for symmetry breaking to be observed in such simulations.

• Random initial states. In cellular-automaton pseudorandom generators [28] and in simulations
  started from random configurations, a seed in Snd confines the whole trajectory to Snd (The-
                                                                                               d
  orem 8.1). For a uniform seed this happens with probability at most (2d − 1 + 2 log2 n) σ −n /2
  (Corollary 7.1), which is negligible except on very small lattices. Structured or sparse seeds
  are another matter (the all-zero seed lies in Snd ). For a uniform seed witha prescribed num-
  ber k of active cells, Theorem 5.1 gives the exact probability |Snad ,k |/ Nk (σ − 1)N −k , and
  Corollary 7.2 gives it when k is itself uniform; the algorithm of Section 9 tests a seed in
  O(sopf d (n) · nd ) cell reads.

have a counterpart on one side only). No link is of the kind “weaker”, which would mark a part
of the paper not covered by a frozen statement. The highlighted regions are computed from an
instrumented copy of the paper whose words must typeset at exactly the positions they have in
the official PDF; every action of the auditor is recorded, with timestamps, in a log kept under
version control; and when the viewer data are rebuilt, a verdict is flagged as stale if the item’s
TEX source, the Lean source of its declarations, or its link data differ from those it was given
against. All 25 items were approved, and their statements are unchanged in this version. A
separate check of the link data, made during the audit, found that one clause, the symmetry-
breaking sentence of Corollary 8.2, which the links had classified as having no Lean counterpart,
was not covered by any frozen statement; it was reworded and frozen before this version, and
its new links were then audited. Tools of this kind make the alignment between informal and
formal statements inspectable item by item, and we regard them as a natural companion to
proof assistants for papers like this one.
